Major Writing Assignment 2 · ENC 1102

Major Writing Assignment: Research Proposal

ENC 1102 · 100 points

Overview

In this major writing assignment, you will write a research proposal for your upcoming ethnographic research essay. Your proposal will identify a specific discourse community you have access to and plan to study, provide a literature review situating your project within existing conversations about writing, rhetoric, language, and/or literacy, and articulate research questions, aims, and objectives for your study. You will describe your research methods (observation, artifact collection, and/or interviews) and your plan for access and ethical data collection, offer a timeline for completing your ethnographic research essay, and conclude by explaining what you hope to learn and why it matters.

Why we're doing this

Researchers rarely just start collecting data. They write proposals first: to think through what they want to know, to find out who has already asked similar questions, and to make sure their plan is doable and fair to the people involved. Your proposal does the same work. It is a plan, not a finished study, so you are not expected to know your answers yet. You are expected to show that you've thought carefully about how you will find them.

Everything you write here feeds directly into your ethnographic research essay. Your problem, literature review, and methods will become the first half of that essay, so the time you spend now saves you time later.

What you'll learn

By completing this proposal, you will practice:

  • Identifying a discourse community and describing how its members use writing, language, and literacy
  • Finding, reading, and synthesizing scholarly sources into a conversation
  • Writing open-ended research questions and turning them into concrete steps
  • Planning ethical research with real people
  • Managing a multi-stage writing project through drafting, feedback, and revision

Choosing a discourse community

A discourse community is a group of people who share goals and use particular kinds of writing, talk, and vocabulary to reach them. Good choices are communities you already belong to or can easily reach: a job, a team, a club, a congregation, a band, a fan or gaming community, a skate crew, an online forum, a volunteer group. The best community for this project is one where you can actually observe, collect texts, and talk to people over the next few weeks.

If you're unsure whether your community will work, that's a great question to bring to office hours or send me by email. We can figure it out together.

A note on ethics

You'll be writing about real people, so treat them the way you'd want to be treated. Ask before you observe, collect, or record. Use pseudonyms. Don't collect anything private or sensitive, and stop if someone changes their mind. If you're unsure whether something is okay to include, ask me first.

Proposal Template

Each section below has three parts:

  • What this section does: its job in the proposal.
  • Prompts and sentence frames: questions to answer and starter sentences you may use, adapt, or ignore.
  • Model: how I answered the same section in my own dissertation preproposal, "Recoding: Decoding as Making/as Encoding." Your project will be much smaller. Use the model to see the move each section makes, not its scale or vocabulary.

Replace every prompt with your own writing. Keep the headings. Write in full sentences unless a section asks for a list or table.

1. Working Title

What this section does: Names your project so a reader knows the community and the question at a glance. "Working" means it can change.

Prompts:

  • What community are you studying?
  • What practice of writing, language, or literacy are you looking at inside it?
  • Could a play on words, a colon, or a slash capture a tension in your project?

Sentence frames:

  • "[Key Term]: [Practice] in [Community]"
  • "[Practice] as [What It Really Does]: An Ethnography of [Community]"

Model: "Recoding: Decoding as Making/as Encoding" (working title)

Notice: the slashes stage the project's argument, that reading culture and making from it may be the same act.

2. Problem

What this section does: Identifies a gap: something about how this community uses writing, language, or literacy that people either haven't studied, have studied only one way, or have misunderstood. The problem is the reason your study needs to exist.

Prompts:

  • What do outsiders, or even insiders, usually assume about how this community communicates?
  • How is this community, or communities like it, usually studied or talked about? What does that approach miss?
  • Which two conversations are rarely put together, and what might happen if they were?

Sentence frames:

  • "[Community or practice] is usually understood as ___, rather than as ___."
  • "Research on ___ tends to focus on ___, while research on ___ focuses on ___. Little research brings these together."
  • "Little is known about how ___ actually ___."

Model: "Corpus-driven and generative work is usually theorized as authorship or procedure rather than reception, while reception studies lean on close reading and largely circulate within academia. Little research brings reception, distant reading, and creation into one conversation, or asks what may be learned when transparent procedures let one distantly read culture and make from it."

Notice: the first sentence names how two fields usually work. The second names what neither does.

Length: 3–5 sentences.

3. Discourse Community and Access

What this section does: Introduces the community you will study and shows you can actually reach it. A brilliant question about a community you can't access is not a workable study.

Prompts:

  • Who is in this community? Where and how often do they gather (in person, online, both)?
  • What shared goals, genres, specialized vocabulary, or ways of communicating mark someone as a member?
  • What is your relationship to it: full member, newcomer, outsider with a contact? What does that position let you see, and what might it hide?
  • Who can grant or help you get access? Have you asked yet?
  • Are any spaces private, members-only, or sensitive? How will you respect that?

Sentence frames:

  • "I will study ___, a community of ___ who share the goal of ___."
  • "Members communicate mainly through ___, using genres such as ___ and terms such as ___."
  • "I have been a ___ in this community for ___, which gives me access to ___ but may also lead me to overlook ___."
  • "To gain access, I will contact ___ and ask for permission to ___."

Model: My study builds its own community: members of the public who join workshops, contribute materials, and make works with shared tools. I am also a participant, so the study includes "reflexive analysis of my own making."

Notice: naming your own position inside the community is part of the method, not a confession.

Length: 1–2 paragraphs.

4. Research Questions, Aims, and Objectives

What this section does: States exactly what you want to find out (questions), what you hope the study accomplishes (aims), and the concrete steps that will get you there (objectives).

  • Research questions are open: they begin with how, in what ways, or what, not is or does. A yes/no question ends your study before it starts.
  • Aims are the big-picture purposes: to understand, describe, or explain something.
  • Objectives are specific, checkable actions: observe, collect, interview, compare.

Sentence frames:

  • Primary question: "In what ways does ___ function as ___ for members of ___?"
  • Secondary question: "What might ___ learn through ___?" or "How do newcomers learn to ___?"
  • Aim: "This study aims to understand how ___."
  • Objectives: "To do this, I will (1) observe ___, (2) collect ___, and (3) interview ___."

Model: "In what ways does making with cultural data sets function as a decoding practice? What might a community of workshop participants learn through it?"

Notice: the first question reframes a familiar activity (making) as something else (decoding). The second turns to what the community learns. Your aims and objectives come from these two moves.

Include: 1 primary question, 1–2 secondary questions, 1–2 sentences of aims, and 3–5 numbered objectives.

5. Literature Review

What this section does: Places your study in existing conversations about writing, rhetoric, language, and/or literacy. A literature review is not a list of summaries. It groups sources into conversations, shows what each conversation says, and shows where your study enters.

Step 1: Name your areas. Identify 2–4 areas of scholarship your study draws on. Usually at least one area is about discourse communities or literacy practices in general, and at least one is about your community, its genres, or a similar community.

Model areas:

  1. Corpus-driven and generative writing, including procedural authorship and electronic literature
  2. Research-creation, practice as research, and critical making, including design justice and data feminism
  3. Reception studies, encoding/decoding, and distant viewing
  4. Pedagogies of making in writing studies

Notice: each area is named broadly, then narrowed with "including." Together, the four areas cover every term in the research question.

Step 2: Map your sources. Use a table like this one while you research (it's a tool for you; you don't need to submit it).

Area Source (author, year) Main argument or finding How it connects to my study

Step 3: Write the review. Write at least one paragraph per area. In each, synthesize sources (put them in conversation), then end with what is still missing.

Sentence frames:

  • "Scholars of ___ have shown that ___ (Author, year; Author, year)."
  • "While ___ argues ___, ___ complicates this by ___."
  • "These studies focus on ___, but few examine ___."
  • "My study builds on ___ by ___."

6. Data

What this section does: Lists what you will gather: the actual materials you will analyze. (How you gather them comes next.)

Ethnographic data usually falls into three kinds:

  • Observations: your field notes on what members do, say, and write.
  • Artifacts: texts the community makes or uses: posts, flyers, manuals, menus, chat logs, playlists, scorecards, signage.
  • Interviews: members' own accounts of how and why they communicate.

Prompts:

  • Which genres or texts are most central to how this community works?
  • Will you make anything yourself (field notes, a reflection log)? That counts as data.
  • Roughly how much of each kind will you gather? (e.g., 3 observation sessions, 10 artifacts, 2 interviews)

Model:

  1. The final creative deliverable
  2. Form data from determining the workshop's theme and tools used
  3. Participant-built corpora: contributed articles, which generate keywords and vocabulary; questionnaire responses, which shape syntax; and contributed images
  4. Works made by workshop participants using my tools
  5. Entrance and follow-up interviews with workshop participants

Notice: each item is concrete and countable, and item 3 says what each kind of material is for.

Format: a numbered list with rough amounts.

7. Data Collection and Ethics

What this section does: Explains how you will gather each kind of data, and how you will do it ethically. Every item in your Data list should have a matching collection method here.

Prompts for collection:

  • Observation: Where, when, and how often? Will you participate or watch? How will you take notes (notebook, phone, voice memo)?
  • Artifacts: Where will you find them? Are they public or shared only with members? How will you save them (screenshot, photo, copy)?
  • Interviews: Whom will you interview, and why them? Structured (fixed questions) or semi-structured (core questions plus follow-ups)? In person, by phone, or by message? Will you record?

Prompts for ethics:

  • How will you ask for consent, and how will you record that someone said yes?
  • How will you protect identities (pseudonyms, removing usernames, blurring faces)?
  • What will you not collect, even if you could?
  • How will you store your data, and what happens to it after the project?
  • What do participants get out of this? Is there a way to share your findings with the community?

Sentence frames:

  • "I will observe ___ during ___, taking field notes on ___."
  • "I will collect ___ from ___, saving them by ___."
  • "I will conduct semi-structured interviews with ___ members, chosen because ___."
  • "All participants will be told ___ and asked for consent before ___. I will use pseudonyms and will not collect ___."

Model: "(1) Process documentation of final deliverable; (2) theme-selection forms; (3) public workshops using the tools, in which participants contribute articles, questionnaire responses, and images; (4) entrance and follow-up semi-structured interviews; and (5) participant works and tool contributions, all gathered only with explicit consent."

Notice: the numbers match the Data list one to one, and consent governs everything collected.

8. Data Analysis

What this section does: Explains what you will do with your data once you have it, and which idea or framework will help you interpret it.

Common methods at this scale:

  • Coding: reading notes, artifacts, and interview transcripts for recurring patterns, then labeling and grouping them.
  • Keyword or vocabulary analysis: tracking specialized terms, how often they appear, and who uses them.
  • Genre analysis: describing the conventions of a text the community relies on and what those conventions accomplish.
  • Reflexive analysis: examining how your own position shaped what you noticed.

Prompts:

  • What patterns do you expect to look for? What would surprise you?
  • Which concept from your literature review will you use as a lens (e.g., Swales's characteristics of a discourse community, literacy sponsors, genre as social action)?
  • How will you check your interpretations (comparing data sources, asking a member)?

Sentence frames:

  • "I will code my field notes and interview transcripts for ___, ___, and ___."
  • "I will analyze ___ by tracking ___."
  • "I will read my findings through ___'s concept of ___, to explore whether ___."

Model: "Keyword concordance, density, and co-occurrence analysis of participant-built corpora, and distant viewing of contributed images; qualitative coding of interviews and participant works; and reflexive analysis of my own making. Findings are read through Hall's circuit, to explore whether making may collapse decoding into encoding."

Notice: each method is matched to a kind of data, and the last sentence names a single framework (Hall's encoding/decoding circuit) and what the analysis tests against it.

9. Timeline

What this section does: Shows that your study fits between now and the ethnographic essay's due date. A timeline also shows which steps depend on others (you can't analyze interviews you haven't done).

Prompts:

  • When will you ask for access and consent? Build in time for people to reply.
  • When does the community meet or post? Schedule observations around their calendar, not just yours.
  • How much time will transcribing and coding take? (Usually longer than you expect.)
  • What is your backup if a meeting is cancelled or an interviewee drops out?

10. Significance: What You Hope to Learn and Why It Matters

What this section does: Answers "So what?" It says what your study will show, who benefits, and what it adds to the conversations in your literature review.

Prompts:

  • What do you expect, or hope, to learn? (You can be wrong later. That's research.)
  • Who could use what you find: members, newcomers, outsiders, teachers, scholars?
  • What does your study add to the conversation you reviewed?
  • Could your findings give something back to the community?

Sentence frames:

  • "This study will show how ___."
  • "It matters for ___ because ___."
  • "It contributes to conversations about ___ by ___."
  • "It may also help members of ___ to ___."

Model: "The study will show how people recode culture by making with data sets, and encourage them to do so, through both my own case and participants' work. It will contribute to research-creation and Texts and Technology a corpus-driven, consent-based method grounded in design justice and data feminism. It will also put that method into more hands through open-source, low-demand tools and workshops for the public."

Notice: three sentences, three audiences: the people studied, the field, and the public.

Length: 1 paragraph.

11. Outline of Your Ethnographic Research Essay

What this section does: Sketches how your final essay will be organized, so you can see the whole project before you write it.

Model (a dissertation):

  1. Introduction, research question, significance, and outline of the study
  2. Literature review
  3. Research design, including research-creation, practice as research, workshops, interviews, and ethics
  4. Results, including reports on tools used, workshop and interview findings, and a creative deliverable (form to be determined)
  5. Conclusion or discussion

Notice: most of this proposal becomes the first three parts of the final project. Your essay follows the same arc on a smaller scale, with sections instead of chapters.

A starting outline (adjust it to fit your project):

  1. Introduction: community, research question, significance
  2. Literature review
  3. Methods: community and access, data collection, ethics
  4. Findings: ___
  5. Discussion and conclusion: ___
